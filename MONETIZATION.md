# Where this output can actually earn

Researched September 2026. Platform policies on AI music are moving fast — every
claim below carries its source, and you should re-check before committing to a
platform. **None of this is legal advice.**

The short version: **YouTube is the only route where this pipeline's output has a
straightforward path to income.** Streaming is possible but constrained. Stock
music libraries are effectively closed. And there is one policy line that lands
directly on `lofify`, which you need to know before you upload anything.

---

## The headline: not all of this pipeline sits in the same place

The single most useful thing to understand is that the three generators put you
in **three very different positions**, and they are not interchangeable when it
comes to policy risk.

| What you ran | Position |
|---|---|
| `lofi` (built-in engine) | **Strongest.** No trained model, no training data, no third-party recordings. It is procedural synthesis — chord progressions, synthesis and DSP driven by settings you choose. That is much closer to "made with a synthesizer" than to "generated by an AI model", both technically and in how platforms describe the category. |
| `generate` (ACE-Step) | **Middle.** A genuine generative model. Apache 2.0 weights, so commercial use is permitted, but it is AI-generated music and every disclosure rule applies. |
| `lofify` (your songs) | **Weakest for upload as-is.** See the warning below. Fine as *source material* you build on; risky as a finished upload. |

### The `lofify` warning

In July 2025 YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" policy to **"inauthentic
content"**, and the policy explicitly calls out *songs that are only
pitch-shifted or sped up* and *tracks built from the same template with
different thumbnails*.

`lofify --preset classic` slows a track to 0.88× and drops the pitch with it.
That is, almost word for word, the named example.

This does **not** mean `lofify` is useless — it means raw `lofify` output is not
a finished upload. What moves it out of that category is the same thing that
moves anything out of it: real work on top. Curate what you keep, build a `song`
with an actual arrangement rather than dumping tracks back to back, put original
visuals on it, and vary genre and mood between uploads. `check` will now remind
you of this when the run was a `lofify` run.

## Platform by platform

### YouTube — the realistic primary

**Verdict: viable, and the best fit for hours-long lofi.**

- YouTube does not ban AI tools. It judges the final video, not the software
  that made it, and AI-assisted content can join the Partner Program if it adds
  value.
- Lofi with original visuals is still reported at roughly **$3–10 RPM** in 2026.
  Generic beat compilations and raw AI dumps are not.
- You must tick **"Altered or synthetic content"** in Studio. `check` prints this
  reminder every run.
- The `inauthentic content` policy is the one that decides whether you get
  monetized. Human curation is what separates a monetizable upload from a
  flagged one.

**What this pipeline already does for that:** `dedupe` stops near-identical
tracks going out together, the `check` ledger stops you re-uploading your own
back catalogue (the usual cause of self-inflicted claims), `song` builds an
arrangement rather than a playlist, and `art` gives you original visuals.

**What it cannot do for you:** listen to the tracks and throw out the bad ones.
That step is yours and it is the one the policy is actually testing.

### Streaming (Spotify, Apple, etc. via a distributor)

**Verdict: possible, with real constraints. Not a volume play.**

- Spotify has **no ban** on AI music and says it supports artists using AI
  creatively. It does not down-rank tracks for being AI-assisted.
- But it requires **disclosure**, and now expects metadata naming **which AI
  model or platform** generated the track. It banned unauthorised voice clones
  and removed **75 million spam tracks** in the year to September 2025.
- Distributors differ sharply, and this is where people get caught:

| Distributor | AI stance |
|---|---|
| DistroKid | asks an AI question at upload |
| Symphonic | requires disclosure in the submission flow; omitting it is a violation |
| **CD Baby** | **does not accept AI content at all** |
| TuneCore, Amuse, UnitedMasters, Ditto | no published disclosure mechanism |

- Tracks get rejected for unclear ownership, cloned vocals, misleading artist
  identity, duplicate uploads, and **low-quality mass releases**. Uploading a
  hundred generated tracks is the pattern that triggers this.

**Practical read:** a curated EP or album of your best material, properly
disclosed, is a reasonable move. Bulk-uploading everything the pipeline can
produce is the behaviour the spam purge exists to catch.

### Stock music / sync libraries

**Verdict: closed. Do not spend time here.**

As of 2026 every major traditional stock library rejects AI-generated
submissions — **Pond5, AudioJungle, Artlist, Epidemic Sound, Soundstripe,
PremiumBeat and Musicbed** all ban or exclude it from contributor programmes.
Pond5 (Shutterstock) states it explicitly, runs detection during review, and
suspends or terminates accounts for repeat submissions.

Only AI-native libraries such as Loudly and SOUNDRAW accept AI music, and they
are a different, much smaller market.

**The one exception worth thinking about** is the built-in engine. Its output is
not model-generated and contains no training data — so it is not "AI-generated
music" in the sense these bans are written about. That is an argument you would
have to make to a library, in advance and in writing, and some will not care. Do
not submit and hope.

### Direct-to-fan (Bandcamp, Patreon, Ko-fi, Gumroad)

**Verdict: no policy obstacle, but it needs an audience.**

No AI restrictions to speak of, you set the price, and the margin is the best of
any option. The catch is that it monetises an audience you already have — it is
a way to convert YouTube viewers, not a way to find them. Worth setting up
*after* a channel has traction, not before.

### Selling the output as packs

Lofi sample packs, beat packs and loop kits sell on Gumroad and similar. No
platform gatekeeper. Same caveat as above: it converts an existing audience.

## The copyright question nobody mentions until it matters

**You probably cannot copyright purely AI-generated music, and prompts alone do
not make you an author.**

The US Copyright Office position is that copyright requires human authorship.
Output generated from a prompt without further human creative intervention is
not registrable and not protected. Works with *meaningful human creative
contribution* can be registered, with a disclaimer covering the AI-generated
portions; "meaningful" is assessed case by case.

Two consequences that matter commercially:

1. **You may not be able to stop anyone reusing your uploads.** If the work is
   not protected, it is not yours to enforce.
2. **Content ID registration becomes questionable.** You generally cannot claim
   exclusive rights in material you do not own, and attempting to is its own
   problem.

Again the built-in engine is in a better position here than a generative model:
you are choosing key, tempo, progression, instrumentation, arrangement and
processing, and the tool synthesises to those choices — which is a far more
ordinary "human using a tool" story than typing a prompt. The manifest in every
run records exactly which decisions you made, which is the kind of evidence this
question turns on. That is not a guarantee of registrability; it is a materially
better starting point.

## If income is the goal, in order

1. **Pick YouTube and commit to it.** It is the only route here with a clear
   path from output to revenue, and hours-long lofi is one of the formats that
   still works.
2. **Lead with `lofi`, not `lofify`.** Better policy position, better copyright
   position, and it does not walk into the named "pitch-shifted or sped up"
   example.
3. **Curate.** This is the whole ballgame for the inauthentic-content policy and
   it is the one step no tool can do for you. Generate more than you need and
   throw most of it away.
4. **Vary everything between uploads** — genre, mood, tempo, artwork. Same
   template with different thumbnails is explicitly named.
5. **Disclose.** Tick the box. It costs nothing and not doing it is a breach.
6. **Run `check --log` on every export.** The ledger is what stops you
   re-uploading your own material, which is the most common self-inflicted
   claim.
7. **Add streaming later, curated.** An EP of your best, disclosed, through
   DistroKid or Symphonic — not a hundred tracks through whoever asks fewest
   questions.
8. **Treat direct-to-fan as the back end**, once you have an audience to convert.

Do not budget for stock libraries.
